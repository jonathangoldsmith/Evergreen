### Typical deal structure at this stage
- Mostly equity: about 0.5–2%, vesting over 2–4 years with a 1-year cliff, with part tied to milestones.
- Exclusivity limited to fitness apps; they keep their shoe and apparel sponsors.
- They actually use the app (real data).
- They co-create something, like a mode or a launch challenge with their community.
- A morals clause in both directions.
- Bring a working app and early users to the pitch.

## 2. Is the following big enough?

**Yes, for this stage.** Niche trust matters more than reach. Martinus's community members are closer to downloading than casual followers. People this size will take equity deals. As a rough guess (not data), one dedicated video could bring a few hundred to a couple thousand users, about right for testing the flags-and-outcomes data.

**What it won't do:** mass awareness.

**Setup that covers both:** one face, plus 5–20 smaller creators and run-club leaders (5–50K followers each), plus coaches using the coach view. Sign someone bigger once retention numbers show people stick around.

## 3. Business model

**Original idea:** keep the app free and charge companies like Amazon or Nike to put their products at the top of the buying recommendations.

**Why not:** the agent explains why every other option lost and cites its sources. Paid ranking would make that explanation untrue and would undercut the "works for you" pitch. The FTC also expects paid placement to be clearly labeled.

**Suggested model instead:**
1. **A free core, forever:** picking workouts, defending the time slot, the weekly plan, injury flags.
2. **Affiliate commission, with rankings that stay honest:** the agent picks on merit; you earn commission from whichever store the user chooses, with disclosure. Thin money (roughly tens of dollars per active runner per year), so it only matters at scale.
3. **A clearly labeled "Sponsored" slot** next to the agent's pick, never inside it. The agent can say "the sponsored option isn't my pick because…".
4. **A premium subscription** (Strava and Runna charge around $80–120 a year): race build-ups and taper, deeper load analysis, Watch extras. **Keep safety features free**, especially return-to-running.
5. **Coaches (later):** sharing with one coach stays free; charge coaches who manage many athletes.
6. **Data (much later):** see the next section.

**Order:** free plus affiliate now → premium once there's steady weekly use → sponsored slot and coach tier at scale.

## 4. The data business

This is **the founder's main long-term interest**. It's the only part competitors can't copy: labeled data on which training patterns led to injuries.

- **Sell a product built on the data, not the data:** license an injury-risk score or API built from flag → swap accepted or not → hurt two weeks later. Raw data stays private. The CEO plan already names "an API that lets other training apps plug into the guardrail" as a vision.
- **Likely buyers, roughly easiest to hardest:** running brands (shoe wear versus injury), other training apps and coaching platforms, college and pro teams and the military, employer wellness programs, insurers (opt-in reward programs only).
- **Get right now:** separate opt-in consent for research use; clean outcome tracking; Apple's Health data rules; privacy laws; wellness-only claims.
- **Apple's rules:** as recalled, App Store guideline 5.1.3 restricts sharing Apple Health data with third parties to health management or health research, with permission. Using it for insurer pricing likely breaks that. **Get a lawyer's read.**
- **Privacy laws:** Washington's health data law (separate consent to collect, signed authorization to sell), California's sensitive data rules, and the FTC's health breach rule.
- **Credibility:** a university research partner and a published study.
- **Timeline:** consent and schema now → 6–18 months of data across a training season → 18–36 months to a published study and a first licensing deal.
- **Effect on the business model:** if data is the goal, keep the free tier big; affiliate and premium keep the company going until the data is ready to license.

## 5. FDA clearance

**Possible for a narrow version, not needed now.**

- "Lowers your risk of stress fractures" is a device claim. "Helps you train within your range" is a wellness claim. The FDA reportedly updated its wellness guidance in early 2026; check the current text.
- The likely path is **De Novo**, which results in a "granted" or "cleared" product, not "approved."
- **Precedent:** Apple Watch ECG and irregular-rhythm features (De Novo, 2018), plus sleep apnea and hypertension notifications later. Cautionary: the FDA's 2025 warning letter to WHOOP over Blood Pressure Insights.
- **What it would take:**
  - a narrow, fixed model (the hard rules in the harness, not the language-model agent)
  - a prospective clinical study
  - a quality system and change control
  - very roughly 1–3 years and several hundred thousand to a few million dollars
- **Worth it when a buyer requires clinical claims:** the military, health systems, physical therapists, insurers, or insurance payment.
- **Plan:** wellness language everywhere now → design the research study so it could double as FDA evidence → consider a separate "Evergreen Clinical" product later.

## 6. University research partnership

Researchers get real-world training data they struggle to collect. Evergreen gets credibility, study design help and evidence.

**Study options**
1. An observational study over one training season (12–16 weeks).
2. A randomized trial, flags versus no flags, subject to ethics board approval.
3. A group study with a college team, marathon training group or ROTC unit, using the coach view.

**Use a validated questionnaire:** consider replacing "Any pain this week?" with the **OSTRC overuse injury questionnaire** (Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center). Researchers trust it, and changing later breaks comparisons with earlier data.

**Contract points**
- **IP:** Evergreen keeps the app and model; the university publishes, with a 30–90 day review window first.
- **Data:** a data use agreement covering de-identified data, how long they keep it, and who owns it.
- **Ethics and consent:** approval from the university's ethics board (IRB) and in-app consent; Apple's ResearchKit can handle the consent screens.

### Local researchers found

**Best fits**
1. **Michael Fredericson, MD (Stanford Sports Medicine):** led the Healthy Runner Project, a seven-year study of bone stress injuries in college runners at Stanford and UCLA. He said Stanford women's injury rates fell by more than half (63 → 28 per 100 athlete-years). That's his own account; the paper wasn't found.
2. **Emily Kraus, MD (Stanford):** directs FASTR (Female Athlete Science and Translational Research, Wu Tsai Human Performance Alliance). Co-author of an international consensus on bone stress injuries. Approach together with Fredericson.
3. **Anthony Luke, MD, MPH (UCSF):** researches running overuse injuries; involved with the UCSF RunSafe clinic.
4. **Richard Souza, PhD, PT (UCSF):** running biomechanics; author on TRAIL-W (knee health in women runners). Has published with Luke.

**Worth a look**
5. **Stanford Human Performance Lab (Scott Delp's group):** wearables and digital tools for athletes. No running-injury project found.
6. **San José State Kinesiology:** a 2024 paper on injuries and biomechanics in advanced versus minimalist shoes, which fits the shoe feature. Lead author not confirmed.

**Cal:** no Berkeley researcher on running injuries or training load found.

**Pitch point: research supports tracking training load over running form**
- A 2025 study of 674 military cadets found running form measured by wearables did **not** predict injury.
- A recreational-runner study found sudden jumps in training load **did**.
- A 2024 randomized trial suggested form feedback alone isn't enough without training-load guidance.

## 7. Funding: STTR and the Defense Department

**SBIR/STTR: renewed**
- Lapsed September 30, 2025; no new solicitations for about six months.
- Renewal signed April 13, 2026, running through **September 30, 2031**.
- Agencies were still issuing guidance afterward; watch for new NIH solicitations.
- STTR requires a research-institution partner, which fits a university partnership.

**Defense Department**

| Program | Fit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MOMRP** (Military Operational Medicine Research Program) | Closest match | Says future injury research will include predictive models for military training to reduce overuse injuries. |
| **CDMRP Orthopaedic Research Program** | Partial | FY26 areas include return-to-duty decision-support tools. Applied awards excluded clinical studies. FY26 deadlines were likely August and November 2026, so target FY27 with a university partner. |
| **Army H2F** (Holistic Health and Fitness) | A possible customer, not a grant | Expanding to 111 active-duty brigades by May 2027. A military abstract says 82% of military musculoskeletal injuries come from overuse; training load is named as a key factor you can change. |
| **Defense small-business grants** (SBIR/STTR) | Unconfirmed | No 2026 topic on training injuries found; check the topic listings directly. |

**Suggested sequence:** university partner → one-season pilot → NIH STTR application → pitch the military with data.

## 8. Decisions to make now in the app

These are small, but much easier to get right now than to change later:
- **Research consent:** a separate opt-in, so the data can be used for research later.
- **The weekly pain check-in:** a validated questionnaire (OSTRC) instead of a custom question.
- **Wellness wording:** app, App Store and marketing copy (including the face's posts) say "train within your range," never "prevents injury."
